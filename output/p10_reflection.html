I answer the five Problem 10 questions from our Campus Customs app: the Ticket 101, 102 and 103 tabs and the Cash tab. Observed marks what the records show, Inference my interpretation, and Hypothetical the new scenarios in questions 4 and 5. Payments are recorded payments inside the simulation, not real bank transfers. Token counts are provider-reported; the dollar cost is unknown.

1. How would I evaluate the agents' performance on each ticket, and why?

I separate what the agents did from what our safeguards did regardless of them: the database refusing duplicate proposals, tools that can only propose and draft, and my approval before any money moved. I credit the agents only for the former.

Ticket 101: a size S Classic Bulldog Tee for Tauhid Zaman, linked to Bulldog Print Co's unpaid $840 invoice #501.

  • Goal: confirm that size S was out of stock and that the only restock record was the unpaid invoice, prepare a payment proposal for me to decide, and possibly draft an honest reply.
  • Observed: three attempts.
    • Attempt 1 failed before any tool call: the provider rejected tool use combined with the model's default reasoning setting.
    • Attempt 2 (Boss, Customer Service, Inventory) made 15 read calls and produced nothing, because no one consulted Accounting although the blocker was an unpaid invoice.
    • After a code fix and stricter routing rules, Attempt 3 succeeded. Accounting proposed payment #1 ($840), Inventory read stock and vendors and rightly proposed no second order, and Customer Service drafted a reply. The specialists re-read the same ticket (get_ticket four times), and the draft exposed our internal product code and supplier invoice.
  • Human role: I approved the payment after the rent; it executed as payment #2 (the −$840 line in the Cash tab), lifting Bulldog's R3 block. Stock did not change, and delivery of the reprint is not recorded.
  • Evaluation: partially effective. Inference: once routing was fixed, the agents framed the right decision, but it took three attempts, and the customer-facing draft needed editing.

Ticket 102: Elm City Properties' $2,400 rent notice for lease #1.

  • Goal: confirm the notice against the lease, prepare the rent proposal, and draft an acknowledgement without promising payment.
  • Observed: one run.
    • The Boss delegated to Facilities and Accounting in parallel, and Accounting proposed payment #2 ($2,400).
    • Facilities called a second Accounting instance, which tried to propose the same rent; the database refused the duplicate.
    • Facilities drafted to the landlord.
    • Because the two Accounting reports disagreed about whether the proposal was new, the Boss delegated a fourth time to reconcile them.
    get_ticket and get_financial_position each ran five times, making this our most expensive run (17 requests, 43,787 tokens).
  • Human role: I approved the rent; it executed as payment #1 (the −$2,400 line in the Cash tab) and moved the lease's next due date to 2026-10-02. The pre-payment landlord draft then became outdated and stayed unsent.
  • Evaluation: effective in outcome, inefficient in process. Inference: the agents produced exactly the needed decision, but the duplicate was stopped by the database, not by their judgment, and parallel work misled its own coordinator.

Ticket 103: the Yale AI Club asks for 20 navy size M hoodies at a bulk discount.

  • Goal: establish supply (8 of 20 on hand), consult Inventory about the shortage, quote between the $22 cost and the $58 list price, and draft a reply with no promised date.
  • Observed: the leanest run (8 requests, 20,678 tokens). The Boss read the ticket, stock, cash and vendors, then delegated only to Customer Service, which proposed quote #3 at $50 per hoodie ($1,000): a 13.8% discount keeping a 56% gross margin. It quoted 20 units with 8 in stock, never consulted Inventory, named no supplier, and its draft triggered five wording warnings.
  • Human role: I approved the quote internally. That is the $0 line in the Cash tab: no cash moved, no revenue, nothing sent, and no acceptance recorded.
  • Evaluation: partially effective. Inference: sound pricing, incomplete operations.

2. For each ticket, how did Actual compare with my Expected plan?

My Expected sections described where each ticket should stand after the agents' run: proposals awaiting my approval, unsent drafts, and the ticket still open. That was never meant as the final state. After my decisions all three tickets were resolved: 102 with its business outcome confirmed, and 101 and 103 as "simulated workflow complete; fulfilment not verified".

Ticket 101. Observed:

  • First delegation: planned to Inventory; in the successful attempt, to Accounting.
  • Delegations: Boss → Inventory occurred. Inventory → Accounting was skipped; the Boss went to Accounting directly. The optional Boss → Customer Service occurred.
  • Tools: every planned tool was used (get_ticket, check_inventory, list_vendors, get_financial_position, list_pending_actions, propose_payment and the optional draft_message), with repeated reads.
  • Deviations: a failed and an empty attempt before success.
  • Post-run state: as planned: one $840 proposal awaiting approval, one unsent draft, size S still 0.
  • Final status: resolved as simulated workflow complete after I executed the payment. The customer's need is unverified.

Inference: the agents reached my post-run state by a shorter route. But attempt 2 showed that, without explicit routing, the same team could miss the blocker entirely.

Ticket 102. Observed:

  • First delegation: Facilities, as planned, with a parallel delegation to Accounting.
  • Delegations: the planned Facilities → Accounting hand-off occurred; two Boss → Accounting delegations were added.
  • Tools: the planned tools were used, but Facilities never called list_pending_actions, the check my plan relied on to avoid a duplicate. The landlord draft was not linked to the rent proposal as planned.
  • Post-run state: as planned: one $2,400 proposal awaiting approval and one unsent landlord draft.
  • Final status: resolved with the business outcome confirmed after I executed the payment. The record predates our resolution-type field and is read as a business outcome from its stored confirmation.

Inference: the agent skipped the very check designed to prevent duplication, and the database caught what the plan expected the agent to catch.

Ticket 103. Observed:

  • First delegation: Customer Service, as planned.
  • Delegations: the planned Customer Service → Inventory hand-off was skipped; the conditional Accounting step never arose.
  • Tools: get_ticket, check_inventory, propose_quote and draft_message were used. list_vendors was used only by the Boss, and no purchase was considered. The draft was not linked to the quote.
  • Post-run state: as planned: one quote awaiting approval and one unsent draft. The optional blocked purchase did not appear.
  • Final status: resolved as simulated workflow complete after I approved the quote. Acceptance and supply are unverified.

Inference: here the deviation mattered most. My plan made "who can supply the other 12?" an explicit step, and skipping it left the quote unsupported for 12 of 20 units.

Across the three: every human checkpoint in my plans held, and no proposal executed itself. The agents reliably stopped where the controls stopped them, and less reliably followed the investigative path I designed.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent and tools, and why?

Observed: coordination produced visible overhead.

  • In 102, parallel delegation caused the duplicate attempt, the five repeated reads of the ticket and the finances, the conflicting reports, and the extra reconciliation delegation.
  • In 101, several agents re-read the same ticket.
  • 103 was effectively one agent's work.

Across five runs the team used 51 model requests and 132,763 tokens.

Inference: one agent holding the read tools plus propose_payment and draft_message could likely have handled 102 in a single pass, avoiding the hand-offs, the second Accounting instance and the conflict to reconcile. For 103, one agent would have had vendor and stock information in the same context, which might have prompted the supply question. A single agent is not guaranteed to notice everything, though. In Ticket 103 our Boss read the hoodie stock itself, 8 on hand against 20 requested, and still let the quote cover all 20 without asking Inventory about the 12-hoodie shortage. One agent reduces coordination overhead; it does not remove the risk of overlooking a relevant fact.

Code-enforced permissions are possible in either design. A single agent could also be given a tool set fixed in code. What our multi-agent design added was different:

  • Separable authority: payments, quotes, and customer and landlord messages sit with different roles, so no one agent can both propose a payment and message a counterparty.
  • Attribution: every proposal and draft names its author role, such as "proposed by accounting".
  • A structure that matches cross-functional work: Ticket 101 genuinely spanned stock, payables and customer communication.

My conclusion: use one well-tooled agent for single-domain requests, and specialization where authority over money or external communication should be divided.

4. Three new problems the current team could solve with the tools we built (hypothetical)

Each problem would reach the desk as a new ticket, exactly as 101–103 did, because runs start from an open ticket. No other record has to be added, and each stops at a person's decision.

A. A customer orders 12 Crest Mugs, which are out of stock.

  • Why it could arise: the database shows 0 Crest Mugs on hand.
  • Agents and tools: the Boss routes to Inventory (check_inventory, list_vendors, propose_purchase), to Accounting if cash matters (get_financial_position), and to Customer Service (draft_message).
  • Workflow: Elm City Gifts ("mugs and small goods") has no open invoice and a three-day lead time.
    • Inventory proposes 12 mugs at the stored $3.50 cost: an estimate of $42.
    • I approve, which authorizes the purchase and moves no money.
    • I execute it separately, confirming the $42 estimate basis and stating that the supplier agreed. That reduces checking by $42, within the $160 available for purchases.
    • Customer Service drafts a reply with no promised date.
  • Why the current system suffices: each step uses an existing tool or approval route.
  • Limits: the cost is an estimate, the supplier's agreement is my statement, and receipt of the mugs cannot be verified, because stock never changes.

B. A residence hall asks for 30 Yale Caps at a bulk price.

  • Why it could arise: group orders like Ticket 103 recur, and caps are well stocked: 40 on hand, $6 cost, $22 list.
  • Agents and tools: Customer Service (get_ticket, check_inventory, propose_quote, draft_message).
  • Workflow: Customer Service proposes a price between $6 and $22 for exactly 30 caps; the tool refuses any other quantity or price. I approve it as an internal price decision. A quote has no execution step: nothing is sent, and no cash moves.
  • Why the current system suffices: unlike 103, there is no shortfall and no supplier question.
  • Limits: acceptance, the sale and the stock reduction cannot be recorded.

C. The landlord asks for confirmation that September's rent was paid, and when October's will be.

  • Why it could arise: landlords routinely ask, and the Cash tab now shows only $160 against October's $2,400. Like Ticket 102, the new ticket would link lease #1, which is what lets Facilities draft to the landlord.
  • Agents and tools:
    • Facilities: get_ticket (lease #1, next due 2026-10-02), list_pending_actions (proposal #2, executed as payment #1) and draft_message to the landlord.
    • Accounting if needed: get_financial_position (2 payments recorded, $160 in checking).
  • Workflow: Facilities drafts a reply stating that our records show the September rent recorded as paid on 2026-08-31, without promising an October date. I review the draft and send it myself if appropriate (R7).
  • Why the current system suffices: every fact is already in our records and readable by Facilities, and no proposal is needed.
  • Limits: the payment is recorded in our simulation, not verified as received by the landlord. The draft's amount and date would be flagged for my review.

5. Three new problems the current team cannot solve, and what it would take (hypothetical)

A. A customer returns a defective Yale Cap and asks for a $22 refund.

  • Why the current team cannot: propose_payment pays only an open invoice or the lease, so there is no refund payment. No tool can return an item to stock or mark it defective. And because sales are not recorded, nothing proves the original purchase. This is a technical gap: refunds are outgoing cash, which the rules allow.
  • Tools needed:
    • propose_refund (Accounting): creates a refund proposal. Like a payment, a person approves it, which executes it against the cash rule (R1).
    • record_return (Inventory): proposes marking the returned unit as defective stock. A person confirms it after inspecting the item.
  • New agent? No. Accounting, Inventory and Customer Service already own these duties.
  • Controls: human approval for the refund; physical inspection before the stock change; and proof of purchase verified by a person, since the system cannot.

B. The shop cannot pay October's rent.

  • Why the current team cannot: the Cash tab ends at $160, and lease #1 needs $2,400 by 2026-10-02. Accounting can propose the rent, but my approval would be refused by the cash rule (R1). The only remedy is incoming money: payment on a quote a customer has accepted (Ticket 103's $1,000 quote has no recorded acceptance), recorded sales, or financing.
  • This is a rule, not a missing feature. Assignment rule R6 says the simulation models outgoing cash only, so R6 would have to change before any tool could help.
  • Tools needed (if R6 changed):
    • record_customer_acceptance (Customer Service);
    • record_customer_receipt (Accounting), which proposes a receipt that only a person can confirm against bank evidence, writing to a receipts ledger kept separate from payments.
  • New agent? No. Receivables belong to Accounting.
  • Controls: no cash counted from a proposal or an unaccepted quote; human confirmation of every receipt; and a Cash tab that reconciles inflows as well as outflows.

C. The Yale AI Club asks for 50 custom-logo hoodies, a product we have never sold.

  • Why the current team cannot: propose_quote and propose_purchase work only for products already in the inventory and pricing tables, and no tool adds one. No tool requests or records a supplier's quote. And under rule R7 nothing is ever sent, so no agent can contact a decorator or confirm artwork with the club.
  • Tools needed:
    • add_product: a new product and price, approved by a person.
    • record_supplier_quote: stores a supplier's written price and lead time as evidence.
    • send_message: sends a draft only after a person approves it. This requires changing rule R7, not just adding a feature.
  • New agent? Yes, a Procurement agent. Sourcing new products and negotiating with suppliers creates external commitments, which is distinct from Inventory's restocking of existing products, and it should carry its own permissions and attribution. Customer Service would delegate to Procurement for a recorded supplier quote, then propose the customer quote from it.
  • Controls: human approval for every product, price and outgoing message; purchases still require separate approval and execution; and delivery still cannot be verified without a receiving capability.

What I take from this as a manager

Inference:

  • Completing a workflow is not achieving an outcome. When I tried to resolve Ticket 101, the app asked me to certify that the customer's problem was solved. I could not, so we added a resolution type that records exactly what I confirmed and what remains unverified. No shirt delivery, hoodie supply, customer acceptance or message sending is recorded for 101 or 103.
  • Managing the AI that built the system was management work too. The development assistant made mistakes that our checks caught:
    • a test that accidentally started a real server;
    • an evidence generator that first attached files to the wrong run;
    • a screenshot record that reported zero events through a wrong selector;
    • a checker that assumed the invoice would stay unpaid.
    We ran Ticket 103 without the evidence observer, so its run files were rebuilt and labelled. Ticket 101's full agent replies were never captured.
  • Evidence and the definition of success belong in the design from day one. Retrofitting them cost effort and left permanent gaps.

I would change four things:

  • delegate payment work sequentially;
  • link each draft to its decision, with an edit step;
  • record full evidence on every run;
  • test real tool use, and track cost in dollars, before going live.

The agents prepared useful work quickly once roles were clear; the reliability came from the controls, the records and the human decisions around them.
